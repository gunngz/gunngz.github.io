### Compute a point estimate {days=6}
- In a random sample of 515 adults, 160 answered yes to the survey question. The parameter \(p\) is the proportion of all adults in the target population who would answer yes.
- Use \(\hat p\) as the point estimator. The point estimate from this sample is

\[\hat p=\frac{160}{515}\approx0.3107\]

- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 2–4.

### Enumerate a sampling distribution {days=6}
- Start with the four heights 63, 65, 66, and 69 inches. Take an SRS of size 2 without replacement. Each unordered pair has probability \(1/6\).

| Sample heights (in.) | Sample mean (in.) | Sample range (in.) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 63, 65 | 64.0 | 2 |
| 63, 66 | 64.5 | 3 |
| 63, 69 | 66.0 | 6 |
| 65, 66 | 65.5 | 1 |
| 65, 69 | 67.0 | 4 |
| 66, 69 | 67.5 | 3 |

- For the sampling distribution of \(\bar x\), plot one dot at each pair's mean. A repeated statistic would give stacked dots.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 7–9.

### Compare the sampling center with the target {days=6}
- In the four-height example, the population range is \(69-63=6\) inches.
- The mean of the sample ranges over the six equally likely pairs is

\[\frac{2+3+6+1+4+3}{6}=\frac{19}{6}\approx3.167\text{ in.}\]

- The sample range is ==biased downward== for the population range in this setting because its sampling center is below 6 inches.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 7–8 and 17–19. Uses the four heights in Practice #2.

### Estimate a tail probability from a simulation {days=6}
- The claim is that the mean mass of the bars is 17 g. A random sample of 40 gives \(\bar x=17.28\) g.
- Under a model with \(\mu=17\) g and \(\sigma=0.75\) g, one of 100 simulated samples of size 40 had a mean at least as large as 17.28 g.

\[\widehat{P}(\bar X\ge17.28\mid\mu=17)=\frac{1}{100}=0.01\]

- A result at least this large is unusual under the claim. This gives convincing evidence that the population mean mass exceeds 17 g.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 10–12.
