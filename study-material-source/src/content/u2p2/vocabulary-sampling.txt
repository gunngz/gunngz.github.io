### Point estimator and point estimate {days=6}
- A **point estimator** is a statistic chosen to estimate a population parameter, such as \(\hat p\) to estimate \(p\).
- A **point estimate** is the ==numerical value== of that statistic in a particular sample.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 2–4.

### Population, sample, and sampling distributions {days=6}
| Distribution | What is plotted |
| --- | --- |
| Population | Individual values for the entire population |
| Sample | Individual values in one sample |
| Sampling | Values of a statistic across all possible samples of the same size, with their probabilities |

- One dot in a simulated sampling distribution represents ==one statistic from one simulated sample==.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 5–11.

### Sampling variability {days=6}
- Random samples of the same size from the same population can give ==different values of the same statistic==.
- The spread of the sampling distribution describes how much the estimator varies across samples.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 5–6 and 20–23.

### Bias and unbiased estimators {days=6}
- An estimator is ==unbiased== if the mean of its sampling distribution equals the population parameter it estimates.
- Bias describes the difference between that mean and the target parameter. Variability describes spread around the sampling distribution's own center.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 17–19 and 23–26.

![Four schematic dotplots comparing centered or shifted estimates with narrow or wide spread.](./diagrams/u2p2-bias-variability.svg)

### Sample size and precision {days=6}
- For the estimators studied here, such as \(\bar{x}\) and \(\hat p\), a larger random sample generally gives ==less sampling variability==.
- A tighter sampling distribution gives more precise estimates. Increasing sample size does not by itself correct a biased sampling method.
- **PPT source** · Day 6, slides 20–25.
