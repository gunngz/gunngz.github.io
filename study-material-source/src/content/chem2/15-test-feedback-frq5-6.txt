### FRQ 5(a): reaction and limiting reactant · 2 points {priority=provisional}
**Given**
A student combines \(0.0400\ \mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{Mg}\) with \(10.0\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.500\ \mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Hydrogen is collected over water at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The water levels inside and outside the collecting tube are equal when a gas volume of \(41.2\ \mathrm{mL}\) is recorded. Atmospheric pressure is \(748.0\ \mathrm{mmHg}\); the water-vapor pressure at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(23.8\ \mathrm{mmHg}\). Use \(M(\mathrm{Mg})=24.31\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) and assume ideal-gas behavior. The collected gas contains only \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and water vapor.

**Question**
Write the balanced net ionic equation. Identify the limiting reactant using a quantitative comparison.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Write the correct net ionic equation with ==balanced coefficients and charge==.

   \[\mathrm{Mg+2H^+\rightarrow Mg^{2+}+H_2}\]

2. Show quantitatively that ==magnesium is limiting==. The required amount of \(\mathrm{HCl}\) is less than the available amount. An equivalent comparison of volumes or moles divided by coefficients is also accepted.

   \[0.003291\ \mathrm{mol}<0.00500\ \mathrm{mol}\]

### FRQ 5(b): gas collected over water · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
A student combines \(0.0400\ \mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{Mg}\) with \(10.0\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.500\ \mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Hydrogen is collected over water at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The water levels inside and outside the collecting tube are equal when a gas volume of \(41.2\ \mathrm{mL}\) is recorded. Atmospheric pressure is \(748.0\ \mathrm{mmHg}\); the water-vapor pressure at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(23.8\ \mathrm{mmHg}\). Use \(M(\mathrm{Mg})=24.31\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) and assume ideal-gas behavior. The collected gas contains only \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and water vapor.

**Question**
Calculate the partial pressure of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and the experimental amount, in moles, of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) collected.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. ==Subtract the water-vapor pressure== to obtain the hydrogen partial pressure.

   \[P(\mathrm{H_2})=748.0-23.8=724.2\ \mathrm{mmHg}\]
   \[P(\mathrm{H_2})=0.9529\ \mathrm{atm}\]

2. Substitute the hydrogen partial pressure, \(0.0412\ \mathrm{L}\), and \(298.15\ \mathrm{K}\) into the ideal gas law.

   \[n(\mathrm{H_2})=\frac{P(\mathrm{H_2})V}{RT}=1.60\times10^{-3}\ \mathrm{mol}\]

### FRQ 5(c): ignoring water vapor · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
A student combines \(0.0400\ \mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{Mg}\) with \(10.0\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.500\ \mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Hydrogen is collected over water at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The water levels inside and outside the collecting tube are equal when a gas volume of \(41.2\ \mathrm{mL}\) is recorded. Atmospheric pressure is \(748.0\ \mathrm{mmHg}\); the water-vapor pressure at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(23.8\ \mathrm{mmHg}\). Use \(M(\mathrm{Mg})=24.31\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) and assume ideal-gas behavior. The collected gas contains only \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and water vapor.

**Question**
Another student uses \(748.0\ \mathrm{mmHg}\) as the pressure of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) in \(PV=nRT\), with all other data unchanged. Would the calculated amount of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) be too high, too low, or unchanged? Justify your answer.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. State that the calculated moles of \(\mathrm{H_2}\) are ==too high==.
2. Explain that water vapor contributes to total pressure. Assigning all of that pressure to hydrogen overestimates its partial pressure; at fixed \(V\) and \(T\), \(n=PV/(RT)\) then gives ==too many moles==.

### FRQ 5(d): magnesium needed for 40.0 mL · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
A student combines \(0.0400\ \mathrm{g}\) \(\mathrm{Mg}\) with \(10.0\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(0.500\ \mathrm{M}\) \(\mathrm{HCl}\). Hydrogen is collected over water at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\). The water levels inside and outside the collecting tube are equal when a gas volume of \(41.2\ \mathrm{mL}\) is recorded. Atmospheric pressure is \(748.0\ \mathrm{mmHg}\); the water-vapor pressure at \(25.0\ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\) is \(23.8\ \mathrm{mmHg}\). Use \(M(\mathrm{Mg})=24.31\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\) and assume ideal-gas behavior. The collected gas contains only \(\mathrm{H_2}\) and water vapor.

**Question**
A redesigned trial must collect \(40.0\ \mathrm{mL}\) of wet gas at the same temperature and pressure, with excess \(\mathrm{HCl}\), complete \(\mathrm{Mg}\) reaction, and no gas loss. Calculate the required \(\mathrm{Mg}\) mass. Explain whether this mass is greater or less than the value calculated when water vapor is ignored.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. For \(40.0\ \mathrm{mL}\), use the corrected hydrogen partial pressure and the ==1 : 1 mole ratio== of \(\mathrm{Mg}\) to \(\mathrm{H_2}\) to obtain a required magnesium mass of \(0.0379\ \mathrm{g}\).
2. State that this corrected mass is ==smaller than the mass calculated without the water-vapor correction==. Water vapor supplies part of the total pressure, so less hydrogen and therefore less magnesium are needed.

### FRQ 6(a): temperature and pressure · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
Use particle-level reasoning in each explanation. For real gases, the compressibility factor is \(Z=PV/(nRT)\).

**Question**
A sealed, rigid flask containing \(\mathrm{Ne}\) is heated from \(300\ \mathrm{K}\) to \(450\ \mathrm{K}\). Explain at the particle level why its pressure increases.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Connect higher temperature to greater average translational kinetic energy and speed, then to ==more frequent collisions with the container walls==.
2. Explain that each wall collision transfers ==more momentum on average==, or describe more forceful collisions, and connect this to ==higher pressure==.

**Given**
Use particle-level reasoning in each explanation. The original graph below shows \(\mathrm{O_2}\) speed distributions at three temperatures; compare samples containing equal numbers of molecules.

![Original mock-exam graph: oxygen speed distributions at 273, 1000, and 2000 K.](./diagrams/chem2-test-frq6-original.jpg)

**Question**
Use the graph to compare the \(273\ \mathrm{K}\) and \(2000\ \mathrm{K}\) distributions. Explain their different peak positions, widths, and heights, and state how their total areas compare.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. State that the \(2000\ \mathrm{K}\) curve peaks farther to the right and is broader. Connect this to ==higher average kinetic energy and higher characteristic speeds==.

### FRQ 6(c): real-gas deviations · 2 points
**Given**
Use particle-level reasoning in each explanation. For real gases, the compressibility factor is \(Z=PV/(nRT)\).

**Question**
A real gas has \(Z<1\) at an intermediate pressure and \(Z>1\) at a very high pressure. Explain both deviations by comparing it with an ideal gas at the same \(n\), \(T\), and \(V\).

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. At the same \(n\), \(T\), and \(V\), intermolecular attractions make the real pressure lower than the ideal prediction.

   \[Z<1\]

2. At very high pressure, finite particle volume, excluded volume, or short-range repulsions dominate, making the pressure higher than the ideal prediction.

   \[Z>1\]

### FRQ 6(d): low-pressure behavior · 2 points
**Given**
Use particle-level reasoning in each explanation. For real gases, the compressibility factor is \(Z=PV/(nRT)\).

**Question**
At constant temperature, a student lowers a gas sample's pressure by increasing its volume and claims, "The gas behaves more ideally because the molecules shrink." Evaluate the claim in terms of particle volume and intermolecular attractions.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Reject the claim that the ==particles become smaller==. Their own volume does not change; it becomes negligible relative to the larger gas or container volume.
2. Explain that greater particle separation weakens the effect of intermolecular attractions, so the gas behaves ==more nearly ideally==.
