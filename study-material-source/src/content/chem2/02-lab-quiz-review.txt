### Experiment purpose
The lab uses two stages:

| Stage | Purpose | Method |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Determine the molarity of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) | Standardize it with \(\mathrm{KHP}\) |
| 2 | Determine the unknown acid’s molar mass and likely identity | Acid–base titration |

**Answer template**

> The purpose of this experiment was to determine the molar mass and identity of an unknown diprotic acid using acid–base titration. We first standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) with \(\mathrm{KHP}\), then used the \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) molarity and volume to calculate the moles and molar mass of the unknown acid.

**Must mention:** ==unknown acid== · ==diprotic== · ==standardization with KHP== · ==titration== · ==molar mass== · ==identification==

### Acid–base neutralization
Under Brønsted–Lowry theory, an acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\), and a base accepts \(\mathrm{H^+}\). In this titration, \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) supplies \(\mathrm{OH^-}\), which reacts with acid protons to form water.

\[
\mathrm{H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O}
\]

In water, a proton is commonly represented as \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\):

\[
\mathrm{H_3O^+ + OH^- \rightarrow 2H_2O}
\]

The remaining ions form a salt.

**Answer template**

> Neutralization occurs when an acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions that react with \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) ions from a base to form water. The remaining ions form a salt.

**Must mention:** ==acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==base provides \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)== · ==water forms== · ==remaining ions form a salt==

### Diprotic acid and mole ratio
A diprotic acid has two ionizable protons per formula unit. For a generic acid \(\mathrm{H_2X}\), neutralization occurs in two steps:

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + OH^- \rightarrow HX^- + H_2O}
\]

\[
\mathrm{HX^- + OH^- \rightarrow X^{2-} + H_2O}
\]

Overall:

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + 2OH^- \rightarrow X^{2-} + 2H_2O}
\]

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2X + 2H_2O}
\]

Therefore, the mole ratio is \(\mathrm{1\ mol\ acid : 2\ mol\ NaOH}\). Count ionizable protons, not every hydrogen atom in the formula.

**Answer template**

> The unknown acid is diprotic, so each acid molecule can donate two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions. Each \(\mathrm{H^+}\) reacts with one \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) from \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to form water. Therefore, one mole of acid requires two moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) for complete neutralization.

**Must mention:** ==two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==one \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) per \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==water formation== · ==1:2 acid-to-\(\mathrm{NaOH}\) mole ratio==

### Titration, endpoint, and equivalence point
Titration uses a solution of known concentration to determine the amount of another substance.

| Material or equipment | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Buret | Delivers and measures \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) |
| Erlenmeyer flask | Holds the acid sample |
| Phenolphthalein | Indicates the endpoint |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) | Base and titrant |
| Unknown acid | Analyte |

| Point | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Equivalence point | Acid and base have reacted in the stoichiometric mole ratio |
| Endpoint | The indicator shows an observable color change |

Phenolphthalein is colorless in acidic solution and pink in basic solution; its transition range is approximately pH 8.2–10. The target is a faint-pink endpoint. The equivalence point is not always at pH 7.

**Answer template**

> The equivalence point occurs when the acid and base have reacted in the exact stoichiometric ratio. The endpoint occurs when the indicator changes color. They may not occur at exactly the same time because the indicator changes color over a pH range.

**Must mention:** ==stoichiometric ratio== · ==indicator color change== · ==pH transition range== · ==endpoint is not necessarily pH 7==
