### Acid–base neutralization
Under Brønsted–Lowry theory, an acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\), and a base accepts \(\mathrm{H^+}\). In this titration, \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) supplies \(\mathrm{OH^-}\), which reacts with acid protons to form water.

\[
\mathrm{H^+ + OH^- \rightarrow H_2O}
\]

In water, a proton is commonly represented as \(\mathrm{H_3O^+}\):

\[
\mathrm{H_3O^+ + OH^- \rightarrow 2H_2O}
\]

The remaining ions form a salt.

**Answer template**

> Neutralization occurs when an acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions that react with \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) ions from a base to form water. The remaining ions form a salt.

**Must mention:** ==acid donates \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==base provides \(\mathrm{OH^-}\)== · ==water forms== · ==remaining ions form a salt==

### Diprotic acid and mole ratio
A diprotic acid has two ionizable protons per formula unit. For a generic acid \(\mathrm{H_2X}\), neutralization occurs in two steps:

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + OH^- \rightarrow HX^- + H_2O}
\]

\[
\mathrm{HX^- + OH^- \rightarrow X^{2-} + H_2O}
\]

Overall:

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + 2OH^- \rightarrow X^{2-} + 2H_2O}
\]

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2X + 2H_2O}
\]

Therefore, the mole ratio is \(\mathrm{1\ mol\ acid : 2\ mol\ NaOH}\). Count ionizable protons, not every hydrogen atom in the formula.

**Answer template**

> The unknown acid is diprotic, so each acid molecule can donate two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\) ions. Each \(\mathrm{H^+}\) reacts with one \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) from \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to form water. Therefore, one mole of acid requires two moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) for complete neutralization.

**Must mention:** ==two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==one \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) per \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==water formation== · ==1:2 acid-to-\(\mathrm{NaOH}\) mole ratio==

### Titration, endpoint, and equivalence point
Titration uses a solution of known concentration to determine the amount of another substance.

| Material or equipment | Role |
| --- | --- |
| Buret | Delivers and measures \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) |
| Erlenmeyer flask | Holds the acid sample |
| Phenolphthalein | Indicates the endpoint |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) | Base and titrant |
| Unknown acid | Analyte |

| Point | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Equivalence point | Acid and base have reacted in the stoichiometric mole ratio |
| Endpoint | The indicator shows an observable color change |

Phenolphthalein is colorless in acidic solution and pink in basic solution; its transition range is approximately pH 8.2–10. The target is a faint-pink endpoint. The equivalence point is not always at pH 7.

**Answer template**

> The equivalence point occurs when the acid and base have reacted in the exact stoichiometric ratio. The endpoint occurs when the indicator changes color. They may not occur at exactly the same time because the indicator changes color over a pH range.

**Must mention:** ==stoichiometric ratio== · ==indicator color change== · ==pH transition range== · ==endpoint is not necessarily pH 7==

### Standardizing \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) with \(\mathrm{KHP}\)
The exact molarity of the \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution is not known at first. \(\mathrm{KHP}\) is used as a primary standard because it has a known molar mass, is stable, and can be accurately weighed. Its molar mass is \(204.22\ \mathrm{g/mol}\). \(\mathrm{KHP}\) is monoprotic, so it reacts with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) in a 1:1 mole ratio.

| Step | Action |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Rinse the buret with deionized water, then rinse it with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to prevent dilution. |
| 2 | Fill the buret with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). |
| 3 | Accurately weigh \(\mathrm{KHP}\) and dissolve it in water. |
| 4 | Add phenolphthalein and titrate while swirling. |
| 5 | Stop at a faint-pink endpoint and record the buret readings. |
| 6 | Repeat trials. |

**Answer template**

> We accurately measured the mass of \(\mathrm{KHP}\) and dissolved it in distilled water. After adding phenolphthalein, we titrated the solution with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) until a faint-pink endpoint appeared. Because \(\mathrm{KHP}\) is monoprotic and reacts in a 1:1 mole ratio with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), its mass and the volume of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) delivered allow us to calculate the \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) molarity.

**Must mention:** ==known molar mass== · ==stable primary standard== · ==monoprotic== · ==1:1 mole ratio== · ==calculate \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) molarity==

### \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) standardization calculation
Example values only; these are not the class’s reported \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) concentration:

\[
m_{\mathrm{KHP}}=0.347\ \mathrm{g}
\qquad
V_{\mathrm{NaOH}}=20.80\ \mathrm{mL}
\]

\[
M_{\mathrm{NaOH}}
=
\frac{0.347\ \mathrm{g\ KHP}}{204.22\ \mathrm{g/mol\ KHP}}
\times
\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ KHP}}
\times
\frac{1\ \mathrm{L}}{0.02080\ \mathrm{L\ NaOH}}
=0.0817\ \mathrm{M}
\]

Use the measured \(\mathrm{KHP}\) mass, its molar mass, the 1:1 mole ratio, and the delivered \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) volume in liters.

**Must mention:** ==convert \(\mathrm{KHP}\) mass to moles== · ==apply the 1:1 ratio== · ==convert volume to liters== · ==molarity equals moles per liter==

### Titrating the unknown acid
Measure the mass of the unknown acid, dissolve it in water, add phenolphthalein, and titrate with standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) to a faint-pink endpoint. Record the initial and final buret readings, calculate the delivered volume, and repeat trials.

In the class procedure, samples around \(0.08\)–\(0.10\ \mathrm{g}\) worked better than larger trial masses because a larger acid sample requires more \(\mathrm{NaOH}\).

**Answer template**

> We measured the mass of the unknown acid and dissolved it in water. After adding phenolphthalein, we titrated the solution with standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) until a faint-pink endpoint appeared. We recorded the initial and final buret readings to determine the volume of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) used and repeated the experiment to obtain reliable data.

**Must mention:** ==measured acid mass== · ==standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\)== · ==phenolphthalein== · ==faint-pink endpoint== · ==initial and final buret readings== · ==repeated trials==

### Water dilution and particle changes
Adding distilled water lowers the acid concentration but does not change the number of moles of acid in the flask. If no acid is lost and the water adds no reacting substance, the same number of moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) is needed for complete neutralization.

| Stage | Main particles or change |
| --- | --- |
| Before adding base | Unknown acid in water; no \(\mathrm{Na^+}\) has been added by the titrant. |
| Before equivalence | \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) reacts with acid protons; acid and partially neutralized particles may coexist. |
| At equivalence | Two moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) have reacted per mole of diprotic acid; primarily \(\mathrm{Na^+}\), \(\mathrm{X^{2-}}\), and water remain. |
| After equivalence | Excess \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) is present and the solution is basic. |

\[
\mathrm{H_2X + OH^- \rightarrow HX^- + H_2O}
\qquad
\mathrm{HX^- + OH^- \rightarrow X^{2-} + H_2O}
\]

**Answer template**

> Initially, the solution contains the diprotic acid. As \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) is added, \(\mathrm{OH^-}\) reacts with ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\) to form water. Before equivalence, neutralization is incomplete. At equivalence, two moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) have reacted per mole of acid, leaving primarily sodium ions and conjugate-base ions in solution.

**Must mention:** ==two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==\(\mathrm{OH^-}\) reacts with \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==water forms== · ==stoichiometric ratio== · ==particles at equivalence==

### Calculate the acid molar mass
Use the balanced equation \(\mathrm{H_2X + 2NaOH \rightarrow Na_2X + 2H_2O}\).

\[
n_{\mathrm{NaOH}}=M_{\mathrm{NaOH}}V_{\mathrm{NaOH}}
\qquad
n_{\mathrm{acid}}=\frac{n_{\mathrm{NaOH}}}{2}
\qquad
MM_{\mathrm{acid}}=\frac{m_{\mathrm{acid}}}{n_{\mathrm{acid}}}
\]

Example values:

\[
m_{\mathrm{acid}}=0.0850\ \mathrm{g}
\qquad
V_{\mathrm{NaOH}}=16.70\ \mathrm{mL}
\qquad
M_{\mathrm{NaOH}}=0.0820\ \mathrm{M}
\]

\[
MM_{\mathrm{acid}}
=
\frac{0.0850\ \mathrm{g}}{0.01670\ \mathrm{L}\times0.0820\ \mathrm{mol/L}\div2}
\approx124\ \mathrm{g/mol}
\]

**Answer template**

> We calculated the moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) by multiplying its molarity by the volume used in liters. Because the unknown acid is diprotic, we divided the moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) by two to determine the moles of acid. Finally, we divided the measured acid mass by its calculated moles to determine the molar mass.

**Must mention:** ==volume in liters== · ==\(n=MV\)== · ==divide \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) moles by two== · ==acid mass divided by acid moles==

### Evidence, precision, and accuracy
Use measured data to support the identification; do not invent individual trial values.

| Measurement | Use |
| --- | --- |
| Acid mass | Numerator in the molar-mass calculation |
| Initial and final buret readings | Determine delivered \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) volume |
| Standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) molarity | Convert volume to moles |
| Calculated molar mass | Compare with possible acids |

\[
V_{\mathrm{used}}=V_{\mathrm{final}}-V_{\mathrm{initial}}
\]

Class results:

| Quantity | Mean | Standard deviation |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Acid mass | \(0.08482\ \mathrm{g}\) | \(0.02035\ \mathrm{g}\) |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) volume | \(16.6776\ \mathrm{mL}\) | \(4.4479\ \mathrm{mL}\) |
| Molar mass | \(124.064\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) | \(7.045\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |

Precision describes how close repeated measurements are to one another; standard deviation describes their spread. Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value; percent error measures that difference.

\[
\%\mathrm{error}
=
\frac{|124.064-126.06|}{126.06}\times100\%
\approx1.58\%
\]

This accuracy comparison assumes the unknown is oxalic acid dihydrate. Repeated trials improve the reliability of the mean, but averaging does not eliminate systematic error.

**Answer template**

> Precision describes how closely repeated measurements agree. Our molar-mass results had a standard deviation of approximately \(7.04\ \mathrm{g/mol}\), indicating variation between trials. Accuracy describes closeness to an accepted value. Our mean was approximately \(124.06\ \mathrm{g/mol}\), compared with \(126.06\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) for oxalic acid dihydrate, giving a percent error of approximately \(1.58\%\).

**Must mention:** ==raw measured masses and volumes== · ==standard deviation for precision== · ==percent error for accuracy== · ==averaging does not remove systematic error==

### Identify the unknown acid
Molar mass alone is not enough to identify a compound. Compare the experimental value with candidate molar masses, then consider whether the structure has two ionizable protons and whether its physical properties match the observations.

| Candidate listed in the class materials | Molar mass |
| --- | ---: |
| Propanesulfonic acid | \(124.16\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |
| Guaiacol | \(124.14\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |
| Amino(imino)methanesulfonic acid | \(124.12\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |
| Propylphosphonic acid | \(124.08\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |
| Pyrazinoic acid | \(124.10\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |
| Oxalic acid dihydrate | \(126.06\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) |

Oxalic acid has two carboxylic acid groups:

\[
\mathrm{HOOC{-}COOH}
\]

Each \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) group can donate one ionizable proton. Oxalic acid dihydrate is \(\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4\cdot2H_2O}\); the waters of crystallization do not add two more acidic protons.

\[
\mathrm{H_2C_2O_4\cdot2H_2O + 2NaOH
\rightarrow Na_2C_2O_4 + 4H_2O}
\]

**Answer template**

> The unknown acid is most likely oxalic acid dihydrate because its accepted molar mass of approximately \(126.06\ \mathrm{g/mol}\) is close to our experimental value of \(124.06\ \mathrm{g/mol}\). Its structure contains two carboxylic acid groups, each capable of donating one \(\mathrm{H^+}\), making it diprotic. It is also a white crystalline solid, consistent with our observations.

**Must mention:** ==experimental molar mass== · ==accepted molar mass== · ==two \(\mathrm{-COOH}\) groups== · ==two ionizable \(\mathrm{H^+}\)== · ==diprotic== · ==white crystalline solid==

### Experimental error and its direction
For a diprotic acid, with volume in liters:

\[
MM_{\mathrm{acid}}
=
\frac{2m_{\mathrm{acid}}}{M_{\mathrm{NaOH}}V_{\mathrm{NaOH}}}
\]

| Recorded quantity changes | Effect on calculated molar mass |
| --- | --- |
| Acid mass increases / decreases | Increases / decreases |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) molarity increases / decreases | Decreases / increases |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) volume increases / decreases | Decreases / increases |

Judge an error by how it changes the recorded measurement compared with its true value.

**Answer template**

> This error would cause the measured ______ to be too high or too low. As a result, the calculated moles of ______ would be overestimated or underestimated, causing the calculated molar mass to be overestimated or underestimated.

**Specific cases**

| Error | Reasoning |
| --- | --- |
| Waiting for a deep-pink color | Excess \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) makes the recorded volume too high, so calculated molar mass is too low. |
| Nonreactive impurity in the acid | The mass includes material that does not react, so calculated molar mass is too high. |
| Acid reacts with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\)-reactive impurity | Reanalyze the mole ratio and reaction; the direction cannot be assumed from mass alone. |
| \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) reacts with \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) after standardization | Its effective concentration changes; using the old molarity causes an inaccurate result, and the direction depends on the timing and calculation. |
| Buret leaks | The recorded volume may differ from the volume delivered to the acid; do not assign high or low unless the liquid path and readings are specified. |

**Must mention:** ==error== → ==affected measurement== → ==calculated moles== → ==molar-mass direction==

### Human errors and incomplete transfer
| Human error | Effect on calculated molar mass |
| --- | --- |
| Overshooting the endpoint | Too low |
| Not transferring all weighed acid | Too high |
| An initial buret-tip air bubble disappears during titration | Too low |
| Incorrect meniscus reading | Depends on the direction of the reading error |
| Funnel left in the buret | Depends on whether extra solution drains into the buret |
| Incorrectly recorded acid mass | Depends on whether the recorded mass is too high or too low |

If only part of the weighed acid reaches the flask, fewer acid moles are titrated, but the calculation still uses the original weighed mass.

**Answer template**

> If some of the weighed acid is lost during transfer, fewer moles of acid enter the flask than expected. Less \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) is required for neutralization, but the original mass is still used in the calculation. Therefore, the calculated molar mass is overestimated.

**Must mention:** ==fewer acid moles enter the flask== · ==original weighed mass is still used== · ==molar mass is overestimated==

### Improve the experiment
| Improvement | Error it helps prevent |
| --- | --- |
| Rinse the buret with \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) | Dilution of the titrant |
| Read the meniscus at eye level | Parallax error |
| Swirl the flask | Incomplete mixing |
| Add \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) dropwise near the endpoint | Overshooting |
| Use a white background | Difficulty seeing the color change |
| Transfer all of the acid | Sample loss |
| Dissolve the acid completely | Incomplete reaction |
| Remove bubbles from the buret tip | Incorrect delivered volume |
| Repeat trials | Reduce random uncertainty and improve the mean’s reliability |

### Further confirmation of acid identity
| Method | What to compare or measure | Limitation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Melting behavior | Compare the observed melting or decomposition behavior with reliable reference data. | Requires an appropriate reference and suitable equipment. |
| pH and titration curve | Use a calibrated pH meter to record pH versus volume of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) and estimate the acid’s \(pK_a\) values. | Results need comparison with reference data. |
| Conductivity | Measure conductivity of the dissolved acid to investigate ionization. | Conductivity alone cannot uniquely identify an acid. |
| Chemical reactions | Compare the unknown’s reaction behavior with expected behavior for the proposed identity. | A matching reaction is supporting evidence, not necessarily unique proof. |

**Answer templates**

> We could measure the melting behavior of the unknown solid and compare it with reference data for oxalic acid dihydrate. A match would provide additional evidence for our identification.

> We could use a calibrated pH meter to record a titration curve and estimate the acid’s \(pK_a\) values. Comparing them with reference data would provide additional evidence about the acid’s identity.

> We could measure the conductivity of the dissolved acid to investigate its ionization behavior. Conductivity alone would not uniquely identify the acid because many acids produce ions in water.

**Must mention:** ==specific method== · ==measured or observed result== · ==comparison with reference data== · ==limitation of the evidence==
