### FRQ 3(a): standardizing \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) · 1 point {priority}
**Given**
In a standardization, \(0.6382\ \mathrm{g}\) of pure KHP (molar mass \(204.22\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) requires \(25.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution. KHP is monoprotic. The same \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution is then used to titrate an entire \(0.3303\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a pure weak diprotic acid, \(\mathrm{H_2A}\). This second titration requires \(40.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). Assume each endpoint exactly matches its equivalence point and both acidic protons of \(\mathrm{H_2A}\) are neutralized.

**Question**
Calculate the standardized \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) concentration in \(\mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}\).

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Use the ==1 : 1 mole ratio== of KHP to \(\mathrm{NaOH}\), arranging the conversion factors so the final units are ==moles per liter==.

\[\begin{aligned}
c(\mathrm{NaOH})
&=\frac{0.6382\ \mathrm{g\ KHP}}{25.00\ \mathrm{mL}}
\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ KHP}}{204.22\ \mathrm{g\ KHP}}\\
&\quad\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ KHP}}
\times\frac{1000\ \mathrm{mL}}{1\ \mathrm{L}}\\
&=0.1250\ \mathrm{mol\,L^{-1}}
\end{aligned}\]

### FRQ 3(b): molar mass of the acid · 3 points
**Given**
In a standardization, \(0.6382\ \mathrm{g}\) of pure KHP (molar mass \(204.22\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) requires \(25.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution. KHP is monoprotic. The same \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution is then used to titrate an entire \(0.3303\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a pure weak diprotic acid, \(\mathrm{H_2A}\). This second titration requires \(40.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). Assume each endpoint exactly matches its equivalence point and both acidic protons of \(\mathrm{H_2A}\) are neutralized.

**Question**
Calculate the molar mass of the acid. Show the mole relationships and include units.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Convert the titrant volume from ==mL to L==, then use its concentration to obtain ==moles of NaOH==.

   \[\begin{aligned}
   n(\mathrm{NaOH})&=40.00\ \mathrm{mL}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{L}}{1000\ \mathrm{mL}}\times\frac{0.1250\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}}{1\ \mathrm{L}}\\
   &=0.005000\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}
   \end{aligned}\]

2. Use the ==2 : 1 mole ratio==, with moles of NaOH in the denominator of the conversion factor.

   \[n(\mathrm{H_2A})=0.005000\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ H_2A}}{2\ \mathrm{mol\ NaOH}}=0.002500\ \mathrm{mol\ H_2A}\]

3. Divide the acid mass by its amount to leave ==grams per mole==.

   \[M=0.3303\ \mathrm{g}\times\frac{1}{0.002500\ \mathrm{mol}}=132.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\]

Key idea: Each conversion factor cancels the preceding unit. Keep unrounded intermediate values until the final answer.

### FRQ 3(c): adding water before titration · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
In a standardization, \(0.6382\ \mathrm{g}\) of pure KHP (molar mass \(204.22\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) requires \(25.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution. KHP is monoprotic. The same \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution is then used to titrate an entire \(0.3303\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a pure weak diprotic acid, \(\mathrm{H_2A}\). This second titration requires \(40.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). Assume each endpoint exactly matches its equivalence point and both acidic protons of \(\mathrm{H_2A}\) are neutralized.

**Question**
Before titrating an otherwise identical sample, a student adds an extra \(20\ \mathrm{mL}\) of distilled water to the flask. Does the required volume of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) increase, decrease, or remain unchanged? Explain.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. State that the required \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) volume is ==unchanged== because added water does not change the moles of acid in the entire sample.
2. Explain the chain: unchanged acid moles require unchanged \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) moles; at fixed \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) concentration, the ==required volume is unchanged==.

### FRQ 3(d): air in the buret tip · 2 points {priority}
**Given**
In a standardization, \(0.6382\ \mathrm{g}\) of pure KHP (molar mass \(204.22\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\)) requires \(25.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution. KHP is monoprotic. The same \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) solution is then used to titrate an entire \(0.3303\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a pure weak diprotic acid, \(\mathrm{H_2A}\). This second titration requires \(40.00\ \mathrm{mL}\) of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\). Assume each endpoint exactly matches its equivalence point and both acidic protons of \(\mathrm{H_2A}\) are neutralized.

**Question**
In another unknown-acid trial, the \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) concentration has been standardized correctly, but an air bubble initially occupies the buret tip and disappears during titration. The acid mass is correct. How does using the uncorrected buret-volume change affect the calculated acid molar mass? Explain the full causal chain.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Explain that part of the recorded volume fills the buret tip instead of entering the flask. The ==change in buret reading exceeds the volume delivered== to the flask.
2. This overestimates the moles of \(\mathrm{NaOH}\) and acid. Dividing the correct acid mass by too many moles gives a ==molar mass that is too low==.

### FRQ 4(a): empirical formula from combustion · 3 points
**Given**
A \(0.6608\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a compound known to contain only \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(\mathrm{O}\) undergoes complete combustion in excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\). The products are collected quantitatively: \(1.320\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.5405\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). An independent measurement gives the compound's molar mass as \(88.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Use atomic masses \(\mathrm{C}=12.01\), \(\mathrm{H}=1.008\), \(\mathrm{O}=16.00\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\); molar masses \(\mathrm{CO_2}=44.01\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}=18.02\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

**Question**
Determine the empirical formula. Show how you obtain the amount of each element, including oxygen.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Convert the collected product masses to moles of their elements. Count ==2 hydrogen atoms per water molecule==.

   \[\begin{aligned}
   n(\mathrm{C})&=1.320\ \mathrm{g\ CO_2}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ CO_2}}{44.01\ \mathrm{g\ CO_2}}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ C}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ CO_2}}\\
   &\approx0.02999\ \mathrm{mol\ C}
   \end{aligned}\]

   \[\begin{aligned}
   n(\mathrm{H})&=0.5405\ \mathrm{g\ H_2O}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ H_2O}}{18.02\ \mathrm{g\ H_2O}}\times\frac{2\ \mathrm{mol\ H}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ H_2O}}\\
   &\approx0.05999\ \mathrm{mol\ H}
   \end{aligned}\]

2. Convert the carbon and hydrogen amounts to masses. Subtract them from the original sample mass, then convert the remaining oxygen mass to moles. Use unrounded intermediate values.

   \[m(\mathrm{C})=n(\mathrm{C})\times\frac{12.01\ \mathrm{g\ C}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ C}}\]
   \[m(\mathrm{H})=n(\mathrm{H})\times\frac{1.008\ \mathrm{g\ H}}{1\ \mathrm{mol\ H}}\]
   \[m(\mathrm{O})=0.6608\ \mathrm{g}-m(\mathrm{C})-m(\mathrm{H})\approx0.2401\ \mathrm{g}\]
   \[n(\mathrm{O})=m(\mathrm{O})\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol\ O}}{16.00\ \mathrm{g\ O}}\approx0.01501\ \mathrm{mol\ O}\]

3. Divide by the smallest amount to obtain the ==simplest whole-number mole ratio==, \(\mathrm{C:H:O}=2:4:1\), giving \(\mathrm{C_2H_4O}\).

### FRQ 4(b): molecular formula · 1 point
**Given**
A \(0.6608\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a compound known to contain only \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(\mathrm{O}\) undergoes complete combustion in excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\). The products are collected quantitatively: \(1.320\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.5405\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). An independent measurement gives the compound's molar mass as \(88.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Use atomic masses \(\mathrm{C}=12.01\), \(\mathrm{H}=1.008\), \(\mathrm{O}=16.00\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\); molar masses \(\mathrm{CO_2}=44.01\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}=18.02\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

**Question**
Determine the molecular formula using the measured molar mass.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Find the dimensionless multiplier, then multiply every empirical-formula subscript by **2** to obtain \(\mathrm{C_4H_8O_2}\).

\[88.1\ \frac{\mathrm{g}}{\mathrm{mol}}\times\frac{1\ \mathrm{mol}}{44.052\ \mathrm{g}}\approx2\]

### FRQ 4(c): finding the sample's oxygen · 2 points
**Given**
A \(0.6608\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a compound known to contain only \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(\mathrm{O}\) undergoes complete combustion in excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\). The products are collected quantitatively: \(1.320\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.5405\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). An independent measurement gives the compound's molar mass as \(88.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Use atomic masses \(\mathrm{C}=12.01\), \(\mathrm{H}=1.008\), \(\mathrm{O}=16.00\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\); molar masses \(\mathrm{CO_2}=44.01\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}=18.02\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

**Question**
Why cannot the total oxygen in the collected \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) be assigned to the original sample? Explain why subtraction from the sample mass is appropriate.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. State that oxygen in the products comes from ==both the original sample and the oxygen added for combustion==.
2. Because the sample contains only carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen, use conservation of mass: subtract its carbon and hydrogen masses from its total mass to find the ==oxygen originally in the sample==.

### FRQ 4(d): incomplete carbon dioxide collection · 2 points
**Given**
A \(0.6608\ \mathrm{g}\) sample of a compound known to contain only \(\mathrm{C}\), \(\mathrm{H}\), and \(\mathrm{O}\) undergoes complete combustion in excess \(\mathrm{O_2}\). The products are collected quantitatively: \(1.320\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) and \(0.5405\ \mathrm{g}\) of \(\mathrm{H_2O}\). An independent measurement gives the compound's molar mass as \(88.1\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\). Use atomic masses \(\mathrm{C}=12.01\), \(\mathrm{H}=1.008\), \(\mathrm{O}=16.00\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\); molar masses \(\mathrm{CO_2}=44.01\) and \(\mathrm{H_2O}=18.02\ \mathrm{g\,mol^{-1}}\).

**Question**
In another trial, some \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) escapes its collection trap, but all \(\mathrm{H_2O}\) is collected and the initial sample mass is correct. Would the calculated \(\mathrm{C:O}\) mole ratio be too high, too low, or unchanged? Justify without proposing an exact new empirical formula.

<!-- answer -->
**Scoring points**

1. Explain that failing to collect some \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) makes the calculated ==carbon amount too low==.
2. The mass-difference method assigns the missing carbon mass to oxygen, making the oxygen amount too high. Therefore, the calculated ==carbon-to-oxygen mole ratio is too low==.
