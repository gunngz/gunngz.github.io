### Limit exists at a point
The left-hand and right-hand limits must be ==equal and finite==.

\[\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=L\in\mathbb{R}\]

- The function value \(f(c)\) does ==not need to be defined==.

### Continuity at a point
The one-sided limits must ==equal the function value==.

\[\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)=f(c)\in\mathbb{R}\]

- This requires \(f(c)\) to be ==defined==, the two-sided limit to ==exist==, and the two to ==match==.

### Differentiability at a point
The left-hand and right-hand derivatives must be ==equal and finite==.

\[f'_-(c)=\lim_{h\to0^-}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\]

\[f'_+(c)=\lim_{h\to0^+}\frac{f(c+h)-f(c)}{h}\]

\[f'_-(c)=f'_+(c)=f'(c)\in\mathbb{R}\]

- ! **Key idea:** Differentiability implies continuity; continuity implies a finite limit exists. The reverse implications do **not** always hold.

### A corner can be continuous
\[f(x)=|x|\]

- At \(x=0\), both one-sided limits equal \(f(0)=0\), so the function is ==continuous==.
- The left-hand derivative is \(-1\); the right-hand derivative is \(1\). They are unequal, so the function is ==not differentiable== at \(0\).

### Continuous but not differentiable · 2003 AB #20
Consider the function:

\[f(x)=\begin{cases}x+2,&x\le 3,\\4x-7,&x>3.\end{cases}\]

Is \(f\) continuous and differentiable at \(x=3\)?

<!-- answer -->
1. **Check continuity.**

   \[\lim_{x\to3^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to3^+}f(x)=f(3)=5\]

   The function is ==continuous== at \(x=3\).

2. **Compare the one-sided derivatives.**

   \[f'_-(3)=1,\qquad f'_+(3)=4\]

   The slopes are unequal, so the function is ==not differentiable== at \(x=3\).

- **Answer: D** in the supplied review.
