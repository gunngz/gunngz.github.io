### How do you calculate a category's relative frequency?
- “The relative frequency for **[category]** is \(\frac{\text{count in category}}{\text{total count}}=[\text{proportion}]\), or **[percent]%**.”
- “In this sample, **[percent]%** of **[sample group]** are in the **[category]** category.”
- Key idea: Relative frequency is the ==proportion of observations in a category==.
- ✓ For a one-way table, use the ==total number of observations== as the denominator.

### How do you compare categorical distributions across groups?
- “Among **[group A]**, **[percent A]%** of the observations are **[category/outcome]**; among **[group B]**, **[percent B]%** are **[category/outcome]**.”
- “The sample proportion is **[higher/lower/about the same]** for **[group]**.”
- “This describes an association between **[group variable]** and **[outcome variable]** in the sample.”
- ✓ Compare ==within-group proportions==, using ==each group's total as its denominator==.

### Should you use a pie chart or a bar chart?
- “Use a **[pie chart / bar chart]** because **[the categories are ==parts of one whole== / the goal is to compare ==counts or proportions== across categories]**.”
- For a pie chart: “The categories represent parts of the same whole, so their percentages sum to **100%**.”
- For a bar chart: “The bars are separated because **[category]** is categorical.”
- ! A pie chart is not appropriate when categories overlap or the percentages do not describe one whole.

### What should a categorical display include?
- “The **[bar chart / pie chart / segmented bar chart]** compares **[count or proportion]** across **[categories or groups]**.”
- Add a title, clearly labeled categories, and an axis or legend identifying counts or percentages.
- ✓ For group comparisons, show ==conditional percentages== and use the ==same scale for each group==.
