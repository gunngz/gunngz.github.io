### What is the reusable frame for a reaction explanation or prediction?
- **C — Claim:** “**[Direct answer or prediction]**.”
- **T — Type:** “This is a **[reaction type]** because **[reactant-to-product pattern]**.”
- **P — Particles:** “At the particle level, **[reactant particles]** form **[product particles]**; **[atoms / ions / electrons]** **[what changes]**.”
- **R — Reasoning:** “Because **[relevant chemical principle]**, **[particle-level change]** produces **[property or result]**.”
- **E — Evidence:** “The **[observed result / predicted result]** supports this because **[connection to the claim]**.”
- **D — Distinction:** “This differs from **[plausible alternative]** because **[particle-level difference]**.”
- ✓ Use only the steps that answer the prompt. Label evidence as **observed** or **expected**; never present a prediction as a measurement.

### How do you distinguish hydrogen and oxygen with splint tests?
- **Claim:** “The gas that **[relights a glowing splint / gives a pop with a burning splint]** is **[O₂ / H₂]**.”
- **Particles and reasoning:** “O₂ supports combustion. H₂ is combustible; after a flame supplies activation energy, H₂ reacts rapidly with O₂ in the air: \(\mathrm{2H_2(g)+O_2(g)\rightarrow2H_2O(g)}\). The exothermic reaction heats and expands the gas, producing a ==pressure wave== heard as a pop.”
- **Evidence:** “The **[relit glowing splint / pop]** is the expected test result for **[O₂ / H₂]**.”
- **Must include:** **H₂ is combustible; O₂ supports combustion; the pop comes from rapid H₂ combustion with O₂ and a pressure wave.**
- ✓ Do not say that hydrogen makes a pop on its own.

### How do you explain a multi-step copper reaction sequence?
- **Claim:** “The copper-containing material changes from **[starting compound]** to **[final product]** through **[sequence of changes]**.”
- **Step 1 — Precipitation:** “Cu²⁺ and OH⁻ ions form an ==insoluble ionic solid==, Cu(OH)₂(s): \(\mathrm{Cu^{2+}(aq)+2OH^-(aq)\rightarrow Cu(OH)_2(s)}\).”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{Cu(NO_3)_2(aq)+2NaOH(aq)\rightarrow Cu(OH)_2(s)+2NaNO_3(aq)}\).
- **Step 2 — Decomposition:** “Heating decomposes Cu(OH)₂(s) into CuO(s) and H₂O(l): \(\mathrm{Cu(OH)_2(s)\xrightarrow{\Delta}CuO(s)+H_2O(l)}\).”
- **Step 3 — Acid reaction:** “H⁺ reacts with CuO(s), placing Cu²⁺ into solution as the solid dissolves: \(\mathrm{CuO(s)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow Cu^{2+}(aq)+H_2O(l)}\).”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{CuO(s)+2HCl(aq)\rightarrow CuCl_2(aq)+H_2O(l)}\).
- **Step 4 — Redox replacement:** “Al atoms lose electrons and Cu²⁺ ions gain electrons, producing Cu(s): \(\mathrm{2Al(s)+3Cu^{2+}(aq)\rightarrow2Al^{3+}(aq)+3Cu(s)}\). This is ==electron transfer==.”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{2Al(s)+3CuCl_2(aq)\rightarrow2AlCl_3(aq)+3Cu(s)}\).
- **Evidence:** “The expected sequence is a blue Cu(OH)₂ precipitate, black CuO after heating, dissolution in acid, and reddish-brown copper metal after adding aluminum.”
- ✓ State each change in order. Distinguish copper compounds from elemental Cu; if the prompt requests molecular equations, include the spectator ions and balance the full equations.

### How do you tell a metal deposit from an ionic precipitate?
- “Both observations produce a solid, so the solid alone does not identify the reaction type.”
- **Metal deposit:** “In \(\mathrm{Cu(s)+2Ag^+(aq)\rightarrow Cu^{2+}(aq)+2Ag(s)}\), Cu atoms lose electrons and Ag⁺ ions gain electrons to form elemental Ag(s). This is a single-replacement redox reaction with ==electron transfer and oxidation-state changes==.”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{Cu(s)+2AgNO_3(aq)\rightarrow Cu(NO_3)_2(aq)+2Ag(s)}\).
- **Ionic precipitate:** “In \(\mathrm{Pb^{2+}(aq)+2I^-(aq)\rightarrow PbI_2(s)}\), dissolved ions combine to form an ==insoluble ionic solid==. ==No oxidation states change==.”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{Pb(NO_3)_2(aq)+2KI(aq)\rightarrow PbI_2(s)+2KNO_3(aq)}\).
- **Distinction:** “The **[metal deposit / precipitate]** forms by **[electron transfer / ion combination into an insoluble compound]**.”
- **Must include:** **electron transfer and oxidation-state change for a metal deposit; insoluble-ion formation and no electron transfer for a precipitate.**

### Why does carbon dioxide change water's pH and make limewater cloudy?
- **Claim:** “Dissolved CO₂ lowers the pH of water; CO₂ can make limewater cloudy by forming CaCO₃(s).”
- **Water particles:** “CO₂ reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which partially ionizes and ==increases hydronium concentration==: 
  \[\mathrm{CO_2(g)+H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons H_2CO_3(aq)}\]
  \[\mathrm{H_2CO_3(aq)+H_2O(l)\rightleftharpoons HCO_3^-(aq)+H_3O^+(aq)}\]”
- **Limewater particles:** “Ca²⁺ and carbonate-containing particles form insoluble CaCO₃(s): \(\mathrm{Ca(OH)_2(aq)+CO_2(g)\rightarrow CaCO_3(s)+H_2O(l)}\).”
- **Reasoning and evidence:** “==More H₃O⁺ lowers pH==; suspended CaCO₃ particles scatter light and make the solution cloudy. The expected evidence is **[indicator shift toward acidic / milky precipitate]**.”
- ✓ Explain the pH change and the cloudiness separately; the acid formation explains pH, while the insoluble solid explains the cloudiness.

### What changes if hydrochloric acid is replaced with sodium chloride?
- **Claim:** “Replacing HCl with NaCl is expected to stop noticeable CO₂ bubbling from CaCO₃ under the same conditions.”
- **Particles:** “HCl supplies ==H⁺==, which reacts with carbonate. NaCl separates into Na⁺ and Cl⁻ in water but ==does not supply H⁺==.”
- **Net ionic reasoning:** “The original reaction is \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)+2H^+(aq)\rightarrow Ca^{2+}(aq)+H_2O(l)+CO_2(g)}\). Without H⁺, this gas-forming reaction does not proceed significantly.”
- **Molecular equation for the acid version:** \(\mathrm{CaCO_3(s)+2HCl(aq)\rightarrow CaCl_2(aq)+H_2O(l)+CO_2(g)}\).
- **Evidence and distinction:** “Bubbles are expected with HCl; little or no noticeable bubbling is predicted with NaCl. Although both compounds contain Cl⁻, only HCl provides the H⁺ needed for the acid–carbonate reaction.”
- ✓ Make clear that the second outcome is a prediction under the stated conditions.

### Why does chlorine produce iodine from iodide, and where does the iodine go?
- **Claim:** “Cl₂ oxidizes I⁻ to I₂, and the I₂ preferentially dissolves in nonpolar cyclohexane.”
- **Electron transfer:** “Each I⁻ loses electrons and is ==oxidized==; Cl₂ gains electrons and is ==reduced==: \(\mathrm{Cl_2(aq)+2I^-(aq)\rightarrow2Cl^-(aq)+I_2}\).”
- **Molecular equation when requested:** \(\mathrm{2NaI(aq)+Cl_2(aq)\rightarrow2NaCl(aq)+I_2}\).
- **Structure and solubility:** “I₂ is a ==nonpolar molecule==, so it is more soluble in ==nonpolar cyclohexane== than in water.”
- **Evidence:** “A violet or purple cyclohexane layer is expected when I₂ is present.”
- **Must include:** **oxidation of iodide, reduction of chlorine, the net ionic equation, and nonpolar I₂ favoring the nonpolar solvent.**
- ✓ The color in cyclohexane is evidence of where I₂ dissolves; it is not the cause of the redox reaction.

### What changes when a catalyst is removed from hydrogen peroxide decomposition?
- **Claim:** “Without the catalyst, H₂O₂ decomposes more slowly, but the products remain H₂O and O₂.”
- **Type and particles:** “The reaction is still decomposition: \(\mathrm{2H_2O_2(aq)\rightarrow2H_2O(l)+O_2(g)}\). The catalyst provides a ==lower-activation-energy pathway== and is regenerated overall.”
- **Prediction:** “Over the same time interval, less O₂ is expected without the catalyst, so the glowing splint may relight less readily.”
- **Distinction:** “The catalyst changes the ==reaction rate==, not the ==net equation or product identities==.”
- ✓ Compare equal time intervals. Do not claim that removing a catalyst creates different products.
